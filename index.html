Balance
A social wall, eventually — for now, the view from here
Concept & Technical Brief — Balance

This isn’t a skills page dressed up as a gallery. It’s the one place on this site built to be looked at, not read.

What it’s for, why it breaks the site’s monochrome rule on purpose, and how the interaction here is deliberately different from every other card on the site.

01 — The reframing

A repertoire of buzzwords turned into a photo wall would have been the safe version of this page.

This page could have stayed a grid of skill-tags and case-study fragments — useful, but easy to skim past without feeling anything. Recasting it as a photo wall does something a tag cloud can’t: it makes the page worth lingering on for its own sake, not just for what it proves.

The images aren’t illustrations of a skill list sitting somewhere else. They’re the actual raw material here — a genuine change in what this page is for, not a reskin of what it looked like before.

02 — Why an infinite canvas

The same pannable, tiled surface used elsewhere in the prototype — the site’s native format, not a special case.

Every major browsing surface in the source prototype already breaks from a scrolling list in favor of an infinite, pannable grid. Balance doesn’t introduce a new interaction model — it just points that same camera at photography instead of text.

That consistency matters more than it looks: someone who’s already learned to drag-pan one grid doesn’t have to relearn anything to use this page.

03 — What a tile is

Full color, full bleed — the one deliberate exception on an otherwise disciplined site.

Every other surface here stays strictly on-system. This page is the single place that breaks that discipline on purpose.

No grayscale filter, no desaturation. Each tile is a real photograph, cropped to fill its cell edge-to-edge, with a small index number as its only chrome. The restraint everywhere else is exactly what makes the color here read as a decision, not an oversight.

04 — What happens on interaction

Hover lifts a shadow. Click opens an invitation, not a data sheet.

Every other clickable card on this site opens a wide poster-style panel — kicker, headline, tag row, related pathways. This page deliberately doesn’t. Hovering a tile just deepens its shadow and lifts it slightly, a pure elevation cue with nowhere to click through to yet.

Clicking opens a small, centered card sized closer to an actual postcard than a sidebar — the photo up top, a caption set in italic serif underneath, nothing else competing for attention. It’s meant to feel like being handed something, not like opening a record.

05 — Real controls, not decoration

Grid size and image gap both touch the actual layout math, not a filter.

Two sliders live in the bottom drawer: grid size, which changes the tile dimensions used by the same panning physics as every other canvas grid, and image gap, deliberately built as an independent control rather than tied to that same scale — so density and size can be tuned separately.

Neither is cosmetic. Move either one and the whole tiled pattern recomputes and re-centers live.

06 — How it’s actually built

One shared grid engine, extended with two small hooks rather than forked.

This page runs on the same panning, inertia, and tiling engine used across the source prototype — not a parallel implementation that happens to look similar.

01
Shared canvas-grid engine

Two small opt-in hooks were added to support this page — a custom tile renderer for full-bleed images, and a custom click handler — both inert unless a page explicitly asks for them.

02
Deduplicated source set

The photo pool was pulled from a longer list that included the same images uploaded twice in different formats. Each appears once.

03
The postcard is a separate object

Rather than resizing the site’s shared poster panel down to postcard scale, this page opens an entirely separate, smaller overlay — so the panel used everywhere else stays exactly as it is.

04
Independent margin control

Image gap required a small addition to the shared grid engine itself — previously, spacing was locked to the same scale as tile size. It’s now a separate multiplier any grid can opt into.

07 — Scope, honestly

What this is now, and what it deliberately isn’t yet.

In scope for this pass
  • Full-color infinite canvas populated with real photography, not placeholders
  • Independent grid-size and image-gap controls
  • A distinct postcard-style interaction, separate from the site’s shared panel
  • Deduplicated from the original source list — one image per photo
Deliberately deferred
  • The social-wall version — aggregating contributions from other people, weighted by relationship preference, not just personal photography
  • Per-photo captions and context, rather than a small rotating set of placeholder lines
  • Contributor attribution, once this becomes genuinely multi-person

Cards Against Bad UX

PFSweb, LiveArea {Pre Merkle Acquisition} | Circa, 2020–21

Cards against bad UX? What?

What do you do when you're working with a sophisticated client, who has a tremendously complex product line and deep inventory, who are accustomed to B2B and B2E operations and dealings, and whom while fluent with their industry acumen, were completely unfamiliar with "the design process"[?]



What do you do when jargon-laden spreadsheets and single-sided meetings net nothing but frustration with the client to the point where they're considering abandoning the contract?


Enter "Cards Against Bad UX"

All I did was 'simplify what we asked'; to shift the conversation away from technical jargon and towards user-centric thinking. By providing different perspectives such as "As a shopper" "As a content manager" or "As a guest user", and pairing it with a simple visual {a card}, I was able to help our stakeholders empathize with their end-users and focus on their needs and experiences.

This approach not only catalyzed communication but also encouraged collaboration and problem-solving from both sides of the table. It allowed our client to see the project from various angles and find common ground, ultimately leading to more effective solutions and preventing frustration or contract abandonment.

On the Visual Framework

Every size on this site — headline, caption, tooltip, label — comes from a fluid type ramp built on clamp(), not a fixed set of pixel values that jump at breakpoints. A headline at 400px wide and the same headline at 2400px wide are the same element, scaling continuously between a floor and a ceiling, rather than three or four hard-coded sizes stacked behind media queries. That's the difference between a site that's "responsive" in the checkbox sense and one that actually feels considered at whatever width it's opened.

Breakpoints follow the layout model, not a device

640px and 900px show up repeatedly across this site, but not because they're "phone" and "tablet" — they're the points where a given pattern's underlying model actually has to change shape: a horizontal scroll-snap track can't survive on touch the way it does with a trackpad, so Journal drops to a 2-up grid with a modal below 640px instead of a cramped, worse version of the same track. The grid toggle stays a real 2-up grid at phone widths instead of secretly collapsing into a list wearing a grid's clothing. Nothing gets a stripped-down mobile mode; it gets the same intent, re-expressed in whichever layout the viewport actually supports.

Isn't that just responsive design, though?A fair question
Responsive means it survives. This is supposed to feel authored at every size.The distinction that matters

Touch and pointer parity, not a fallback

Every drag interaction on this site — the ice-cube physics, the glyph specimen's resizable split, Balance's infinite canvas — is built on Pointer Events, one code path for mouse, trackpad, and touch alike. A phone doesn't get a simplified substitute for the interaction; it gets the actual interaction, because the browser was already treating "pointer" as one concept instead of three.

On Typography Carrying the Weight

Every case study gets its own typeface pairing. Every letter in Journal gets a different one. The size slider isn't a gimmick — it's the same lever a print designer pulls, just given to the reader instead of kept behind the scenes. Type here isn't decorating the content; in several places (the glyph specimen, the weight ramp) it is the content, doing double duty as both craft demonstration and actual case-study material.

Why that's a defensible bet, not just a preference

A portfolio built around photography or video ages the moment the work does. A portfolio built around typographic systems — pairing, scale, rhythm, hierarchy — stays legible as a skill regardless of which client logos happen to be attached to it that year.

On User Agency

Most portfolio sites hand you a fixed experience and expect you to accept it as-is. This one hands you actual controls, on purpose — not as an accessibility afterthought, but as the argument itself: a designer who trusts the audience with a slider is making a claim about how they'd trust a client with an ambiguous brief, too.

Why List, Grid, and now Feed

List is the honest default: a résumé that admits it's a résumé. Grid reads as a portfolio wall instead of a document. Feed unfolds everything at once, no hovering required — the same reasoning Balance's gallery got a Feed mode.

Same page as Work — this card exists separately because the grid deserves its own conversation. List and Grid aren't two skins on one layout; they're two different state machines that happen to share a data source, and treating them that way is what keeps either one from quietly breaking the other.

State, not just style

Switching modes doesn't just add a class and let CSS do the rest. setWorkLayout(mode) recomputes an actual column count from the current size-slider value, because "how many cards fit per row" is a real layout decision, not a fixed number — Grid at a small type scale wants four columns; Grid at a large one wants one. The toggle and the slider aren't independent controls, they're reading the same state.

Where "responsive" actually gets tested

The honest failure mode of a toggle like this is a narrow viewport quietly collapsing Grid back into something that's technically two columns but reads like a squeezed List — same problem, different name. Grid stays a real, legible 2-up grid all the way down to 512px before it's allowed to fall back to List, and that threshold was a deliberate line, not wherever the layout happened to break first.
